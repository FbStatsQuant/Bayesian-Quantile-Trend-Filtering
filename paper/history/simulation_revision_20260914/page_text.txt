Abstract
Quantile trend filtering combines the check loss with anℓ 1 difference penalty. We
develop a Bayesian extension of its ADMM-based optimization formulation, learning
the regularization strength and likelihood scale jointly with the trend. A three-block
Gibbs scheme combines conjugate scalar updates with a proximal Metropolis-adjusted
Langevin trend update, using ADMM to solve the proximal subproblems. The hierar-
chy uses one global penalty per quantile rather than a vector of random local shrinkage
scales. The constrained proximal system permits reuse of a banded factorization and
linear work in the sample size for a fixed inner iteration budget.
Joint estimation incorporates non-crossing constraints through box projections and
sequential quantile updates. Auxiliary sentinel quantiles provide dynamic bounds for
extreme target levels. Simulations use varying-smoothness and smooth signals under
Gaussian, Beta, and mixed-normal noise. At interior levels, the sampler is competitive
with the horseshoe benchmark in the Gaussian and mixed-normal varying-smoothness
settings, and improves on both Bayesian competitors in most smooth-signal configu-
rations. Its smooth-signal errors are of a similar magnitude to the available ADMM
results.
The method provides a compromise between efficient point optimization and the
more elaborate locally adaptive Gibbs hierarchy: it adds posterior sampling to the
former and simplifies the latter’s computational structure. Incomplete ADMM replica-
tions and limitations of the reported intervals qualify the comparison; posterior output
alone does not establish calibration.
Keywords:quantile trend filtering, Bayesian inference, proximal MCMC, ADMM, non-
crossing quantiles, asymmetric Laplace distribution, Gibbs sampling, extreme quantiles.

1 Introduction
Time-varying conditional quantiles describe how the lower tail, center, and upper tail of a
response evolve across an ordered domain. They are useful when changes in variability or
asymmetry matter as much as changes in location, as in environmental monitoring, growth
assessment, and economic time series. Quantile trend filtering combines the check loss of
Koenker and Bassett (1978) with anℓ 1 penalty on discrete differences, adapting a piecewise
polynomial fit to local changes in the signal (Tibshirani, 2014; Madrid Padilla and Chatterjee,
2022).
Brantley et al. (2020) developed a quantile trend filtering approach for smoothly varying
baseline drift, including joint non-crossing estimation, smoothing-parameter selection, and
an alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) algorithm. Their formulation pro-
vides an effective optimization-based benchmark, especially for the smooth-baseline setting
that motivates the method. At a selected regularization level, however, the optimizer sup-
plies point estimates; posterior simulation requires a probabilistic model and an inference
procedure beyond that optimization step.
We develop a Bayesian extension that retains this computational connection. Under
an asymmetric Laplace working likelihood and a global Laplace prior on trend differences,
the conditional posterior mode at unit likelihood scale and fixed regularization parameter
coincides with the frequentist quantile trend filtering solution. We instead infer the regular-
ization strength and likelihood scale jointly with the trend. The resulting posterior mean
need not equal the optimizer, but both arise from the same check-loss and difference-penalty
structure. Section 2.2 states this connection explicitly.
Related methods include Bayesian and calibrated loss-based quantile smoothing. Fasi-
olo et al. (2021) developed fast calibrated additive quantile regression. For quantile trend
filtering, Onizuka et al. (2024) combined an asymmetric Laplace likelihood with shrinkage
priors and developed both Gibbs sampling and calibrated variational approximations. Their
horseshoe prior supports coefficient-specific shrinkage, allowing the amount of regulariza-
tion to vary across the trend. The supplied Gibbs implementation uses latent-variable and
local-scale updates together with a changing Gaussian precision matrix; its dense matrix
operations motivate an alternative computational route for long signals. This observation
concerns that implementation, rather than an unavoidable complexity bound for all Gaussian
Gibbs samplers.
Proximal MCMC provides a way to construct such an alternative for nonsmooth models
(Pereyra, 2016). In mean trend filtering, Heng et al. (2023) used proximal Bayesian com-
putation to learn the trend, scale, and regularization parameters. Quantile trend filtering
introduces an additional nonsmooth term through the check loss. We use a three-block Gibbs
scheme: conjugate updates for a global regularization parameterλand likelihood scaleσ, fol-
lowed by a proximal Metropolis-adjusted Langevin (P-MALA) update of the trend. ADMM
solves the proximal subproblems. Each quantile has only two sampled scalar hyperparame-
ters in addition to its trend, without observation-level likelihood augmentation or separate
random scales for every trend difference.
The same construction extends to joint estimation under non-crossing constraints. Quan-
tiles are updated sequentially, with box constraints determined by the current neighboring
trends. These constraints enter the proximal ADMM solver through an elementwise pro-

jection. Its fixed coefficient matrix permits reuse of a banded factorization; with sparse
operations and a fixed inner iteration budget, the work per outer iteration is linear in the
sample size. The cost per effective posterior draw also depends on mixing and the accuracy
of the proximal solve.
At extreme probability levels, gradient-based quantile sampling can be difficult. We
introduce auxiliary sentinel quantiles outside the target range, using the non-crossing con-
straints to bound the target dynamically during sampling. This is a practical sampling
strategy whose performance must be assessed empirically. The retained extreme-quantile re-
sults show that its accuracy depends on the signal and noise distribution; narrow constrained
intervals alone do not establish calibrated uncertainty.
Our simulation study focuses on two established benchmarks: the varying-smoothness
signal used by Onizuka et al. (2024) and the sinusoidal smooth signal used by Brantley et al.
(2020). Both papers considered Gaussian, Beta, and mixed-normal noise; our exact noise
parameterization follows Onizuka et al. We compare posterior-mean estimates from the in-
dividual sampler with the two Onizuka MCMC variants and the ADMM point estimator
at quantile levels from 0.05 to 0.95. The horseshoe sampler is strongest on the varying-
smoothness benchmark, while our sampler improves on both Bayesian competitors in most
smooth-signal configurations. ADMM has lower reported smooth-signal errors, but the sam-
pler attains errors of a similar magnitude while producing posterior draws rather than only
an optimizer. The partial ADMM run and limitations of the interval summaries are reported
with the results.
The proposed sampler is thus a compromise between computationally efficient optimiza-
tion and a more elaborate locally adaptive Bayesian hierarchy. Relative to ADMM as a
standalone estimator, it adds posterior sampling and learning of the regularization and like-
lihood scales. Relative to the supplied Onizuka Gibbs implementation, it replaces a vector
of local shrinkage scales with one global penalty per quantile and uses reusable proximal
computations to address the scalability bottleneck. Its competitive Gaussian and mixed-
normal VS results and smooth-signal accuracy illustrate this tradeoff. The contribution is
an intermediate approach that preserves useful estimation performance while offering both
a simpler sampling hierarchy and posterior output, rather than a claim of uniformly smaller
losses or automatically calibrated intervals.
Section 2 presents the model, individual and joint samplers, ADMM proximal operator,
and sentinel construction. Section 3 reports the simulation study, with additional tables in
Appendix A. Section 4 presents the data applications, and Section 5 discusses the findings
and limitations.

2.5 Sentinel Quantile Strategy for Extreme Quantile Estimation
A fundamental difficulty in Bayesian sampling for extreme quantile trend filtering arises
from the behavior of the ALD likelihood gradient. For a quantile levelτclose to zero or
one, nearly all residualsy i −θ i have the same sign, and the gradient of the smoothed check
loss∇ ˜f(θ) approaches a constant vector. The P-MALA proposal then carries negligible
gradient information and degenerates toward a random walk. Because the posterior at
extreme quantile levels has a heavy tail in the direction away from the bulk of the data, the
Markov chain occasionally accepts large moves in that direction, causing persistent upward
or downward drift. For the 0.99 quantile withn= 400, this pathology produces large RMSE
values under individual estimation, rendering the estimate essentially useless. Onizuka et al.
(2024) document degraded coverage atτ∈ {0.05,0.95}across their simulation study, which
includes the varying-smoothness benchmark under Gaussian, Beta, and mixed-normal noise.
The sentinel quantile strategy resolves this by exploiting the sequential box constraint
structure of the joint sampler. We augment the set of quantiles of inferential interest with
auxiliary sentinel quantiles placed beyond the target range. The sentinels are treated as full
members of the joint posterior: they are sampled by the same Gibbs scheme, receive their
own regularization and scale parameters, and their estimates are computable. However they
are not primary targets of inference; their role is structural.
Concretely, to estimate the 0.99 quantile stably, we include sentinel quantiles at 0.995
and 0.999. The 0.995 sentinel provides a dynamic upper wall for the 0.99 quantile through
the box constraintθ 0.99,i ≤θ 0.995,i, and is itself controlled from above by the 0.999 sentinel
throughθ 0.995,i ≤θ 0.999,i. A stable scaffold of lower quantiles (e.g., 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95,
0.97, 0.98) anchors the structure from below, preventing downward drift of the 0.99 quantile.
The result is that the 0.99 quantile is sandwiched within a dynamically updating interval at
every Gibbs iteration, which confines its exploration to a statistically sensible region without
imposing any fixed constraint on its value. The strategy is computationally free in the sense
that adding sentinel quantiles increases the number of sequential Gibbs steps by the number
of sentinels, which is small, without changing the per-step cost.

Interpretation across the two signals.The individual sampler provides a compromise
between two useful approaches. ADMM offers efficient point estimation for smooth baselines,
while Onizuka’s horseshoe Gibbs sampler offers local adaptation through a larger shrinkage
hierarchy and incurs substantial matrix-computation costs in the supplied implementation.
Our sampler uses one global penalty per quantile and ADMM-based proximal updates to
obtain posterior draws with a simpler computational structure. Its S losses are of a similar
16
PAGE 17
magnitude to the available ADMM losses, and its Gaussian and mixed-normal VS errors re-
main competitive with the horseshoe despite the reduction in random shrinkage parameters.
These results support the compromise in estimation accuracy; computational efficiency per
effective draw must also account for mixing and the number of inner ADMM iterations.
Posterior output is an additional capability, not by itself a guarantee of reliable un-
certainty quantification. Appendix Tables 4 and 6 report interval widths without treating
narrowness as a measure of calibration. In particular, the source run reports zero-width
intervals for the individual sampler in 45% of VS and 60% of S replications under Beta noise
atτ= 0.05; the small MCIW values in those cells require caution.
3.2 Joint sampler for extreme quantiles
The joint sequential sampler of Section 2.3, equipped with the sentinel construction of Sec-
tion 2.5, is evaluated atτ∈ {0.01,0.02,0.98,0.99}. These previously reported extreme-
quantile experiments usen= 400 and 100 replications, and are distinct from the updated
n= 500 interior-quantile comparison. The competitors are the two Onizuka MCMC vari-
ants, which fit each quantile independently. Complete VS and S results are retained in
Appendix A.2.
3.2.1 Varying smoothness
For VS, the joint sampler lies between the two Onizuka variants in Gaussian MSE. Under
mixed-normal noise it has smaller MSE than the horseshoe atτ= 0.02,0.98,0.99, and the
two have the same displayed MSE atτ= 0.01. Beta-tail results are more mixed. Tables 7,
8, and 9 give the complete comparison.
3.2.2 Smooth signal
For S, the horseshoe has lower extreme-quantile MSE than the joint sampler under Gaussian
and mixed-normal noise, and both Onizuka variants have lower MSE under Beta noise. The
joint sampler improves on the Laplace variant at every mixed-normal level. These results,
reported in Tables 10, 11, and 12, do not show a uniform advantage from the sentinel
construction.
The joint sampler’s narrow intervals must be interpreted in the context of its non-crossing
constraints. Neighboring quantiles restrict the target’s admissible range during sampling, so
interval widths alone do not show that its uncertainty statements are better calibrated than
those of the independent Onizuka fits. The MCIW results in Appendix A.2 are descriptive
summaries of these different posterior constructions.

4.1 Air Quality Sensor Data
Data.Our first application revisits the fenceline air-quality monitoring problem that mo-
tivated quantile trend filtering in Brantley et al. (2020). The U.S. Environmental Protection
17
PAGE 18
Agency deploys low-cost SPod sensors on the perimeter of industrial facilities to detect fugi-
tive emissions of volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Each SPod records, once per second,
the raw signal of a photoionization detector (PID): a UV lamp ionizes VOC molecules in
the sampled air and the resulting current, reported in uncalibrated counts, is proportional
to the total concentration of ionizable compounds. The series has three components with
sharply different time scales. Emission plumes crossing the sensor produce large one-sided
spikes lasting seconds to a few minutes; the electronic baseline drifts slowly over hours with
temperature, humidity, and lamp condition; and high-frequency instrument noise rides on
top of both. The scientific interest lies exclusively in the spikes, but because each unit’s
baseline drifts idiosyncratically and the counts are not calibrated across units, the drifting
baseline must be estimated and removed before events can be flagged or compared across
sensors. Since the plumes are strictly one-sided excursions above the baseline, the baseline
is identified by alowconditional quantile of the series, making this a natural quantile trend
filtering problem in which, in contrast to the usual convention, the trend is the nuisance and
the residual carries the signal. We analyze one day of data, June 15, 2018, from sensord
of the two collocated SPods in the EPA Rubbertown dataset accompanying Brantley et al.
(2020), aggregating the 86,400 raw 1-Hz observations into 10-second block means, which
yieldsn= 8,640 observations and attenuates the strong serial correlation of the 1-Hz noise
that the ALD working likelihood does not model.
Model and settings.We estimate the baseline at the three levelsτ∈ {0.01,0.05,0.1}
withk= 2, so the prior favours piecewise quadratic trends, each level fitted independently
by the individual sampler of Section 2.2 on the standardized series. The sampler is run
for 6,000 Gibbs iterations with a burn-in of 1,000, proximal parameterγ= 0.1, smoothing
parameterϵ= 0.01, priorsλ∼Gamma(1,10) andσ∼IG(0.01,0.01), and a step size
adapted during burn-in toward acceptance rate 0.5. BecauseS D(θ) is small for the smooth
baselines supported by these data, the rate parameter of the Gamma prior effectively pins
the posterior ofλnear (n−k−1)/10≈864, so the prior acts as a smoothness dial; we
return to this point below. As the frequentist benchmark we use the ADMM solver of the
detrendrpackage of Brantley et al. (2020) with the smoothing parameter selected by eBIC
over the gridλ∈exp{−2, . . . ,12}, which selectsλ= 54.6 atτ= 0.01 andλ= 1096.6 at
τ∈ {0.05,0.1}. Following Brantley et al. (2020), event detection after baseline removal is a
threshold rule on the detrended seriesy− ˆ︁θ: a point is classified as signal when it exceeds
the empirical 90th, 95th, or 99th percentile of the detrended data themselves, so that no
absolute calibration of the counts is required.

pler; the corresponding ADMM fits, and the detrended series they produce, are reported in
Appendix B.1. Both methods track the overnight rise, the large morning excursion around

fit: eBIC selects a twenty-fold smaller penalty at this level than at the other two, and the

5 Discussion
Summary of findings.The individual sampler occupies a useful middle ground between
ADMM point optimization and Onizuka’s more elaborate locally adaptive Gibbs hierarchy.
One global penalty per quantile replaces a vector of local shrinkage scales, and ADMM
provides the proximal computations needed for posterior simulation. The resulting sam-
pler offers posterior draws beyond the standalone optimizer while addressing the matrix-
computation bottleneck in the supplied Gibbs implementation. Its Gaussian and mixed-
normal VS results remain competitive with the horseshoe, although the horseshoe has the
lowest reported MSE and MAD throughout that benchmark. For S, the individual sampler
improves on both Onizuka variants at every Gaussian and mixed-normal level and at all
but the lowest Beta level. ADMM is well suited to the smooth-baseline setting that moti-
vated Brantley et al. (2020), and has the lowest reported S losses; the individual sampler
obtains errors of a similar magnitude through sampling. Its VS losses are lower than those

Scalability.A central motivation for the proposed approach is to make Bayesian inference
practical while retaining the optimization structure of frequentist quantile trend filtering.
The supplied Gibbs implementation of Onizuka et al. (2024) forms a dense inverse and
uses dense Gaussian simulation at each iteration. Our audit found that its sparse option
still enters this dense computational path in the benchmark environment, so enabling that
option does not remove the principal bottleneck. The augmented hierarchy also updates
observation-level latent variables and local prior scales, whereas our formulation samples
one global regularization parameter and one likelihood scale per quantile in addition to the
trend.
This distinction should be separated from an asymptotic claim about all Gibbs implemen-
tations. At fixed difference order, a Gaussian draw based directly on the banded precision
can be computed inO(n) work without forming an inverse, although the changing weights
generally require a new numerical factorization each iteration. For our constrained proxi-
mal system,A=γ −1I+ρD ⊤D+ρIis independent of the sampled parameters, allowing
factor reuse whenD,γ, andρremain fixed. Sparse operations giveO(nJ) work forJinner
ADMM sweeps, plus linear setup and sufficient-statistic calculations. Practical comparisons
must account for the inner iteration count, factorization reuse in the actual implementation,
and mixing of the complete chain. Timing per outer iteration alone does not establish cost
per effective posterior draw.

Role of the scale parameter.Treating the ALD scaleσas an unknown parameter rather
than fixing it at the optimization convention of unity is a substantive modeling decision with
several consequences. Within the Gibbs scheme,σandλinteract with the trend estimate
in complementary ways:σcalibrates the effective likelihood temperature, controlling how
sharply the gradient of the smoothed check loss responds to residuals, whileλcalibrates the
effective smoothness penalty. Decoupling these two sources of regularization allows each to
adapt to a different aspect of the data — noise level and signal roughness respectively — and
prevents the single tuning parameter of the frequentist formulation from having to absorb
both roles simultaneously. Learningσallows the likelihood scale to respond to the data, but
does not by itself guarantee frequentist coverage when the asymmetric Laplace likelihood
is misspecified. Posterior sampling avoids the additional mean-field approximation used by
VB; coverage remains a separate question, as recognized by the calibration approach of
Onizuka et al. (2024). A practical caveat is thatσandλare not fully separable in their
effects on the posterior forθ: both appear in the effective curvature seen by the P-MALA
proposal, and under severe misspecification their joint posterior may exhibit a soft ridge. The
likelihood-scale prior can regularize this interaction, but sensitivity to its parameters remains
an empirical question. The updated interior-quantile experiment usesσ∼IG(0.01,0.01), as
stated in Section 3.1.
Limitations.A single global penalty is a deliberate simplification relative to coefficient-
specific shrinkage. The VS results favor the horseshoe, whereas the individual sampler is
more competitive on the globally smooth S signal. These two experiments do not establish
a universally preferred prior or difference order. The uncertainty summaries also require
further validation: narrow MCIW values do not imply calibrated intervals, and the individual
sampler produces zero-width intervals in a substantial fraction of the lowest-Beta-quantile
replications. Fixed step sizes and finite chains make mixing diagnostics and sensitivity
analysis essential before treating the intervals as reliable uncertainty statements.
The sentinel strategy as a heuristic.The sentinel quantile construction introduced
in Section 2.5 resolves the gradient degeneracy of P-MALA at extreme quantile levels and
produces stable estimates in the simulation study. It is, however, a practical device rather
than a formally justified procedure. The specific recommendations — up to five sentinels per
side, equally spaced within each side, with spacings chosen to fill the available probability
room — are motivated by empirical experience and by the requirement that the sentinels
be close enough to the target to provide a tight dynamic bound while remaining far enough
apart to avoid degeneracy among themselves.
Extensions.Several directions for extension follow naturally from the present work. The
P-MALA framework accommodates non-Euclidean preconditioning through the proximal
operator, and a preconditioned variant exploiting the banded structure ofD ⊤Dmay improve
mixing at higher difference orders without increasing per-iteration cost. The sentinel strategy
is applicable to any gradient-based sampler for quantile estimation and could be combined
with locally adaptive priors, potentially recovering the accuracy advantages of the horseshoe
prior at extreme quantile levels while retaining the computational structure of the present

approach. Extension to multivariate or spatially indexed signals requires generalization of
the difference operatorDto non-chain graph structures, for which the banded linear system
no longer applies directly; spectral or multigrid solvers may serve a similar role in those
settings. Finally, the theoretical properties of the P-MALA approximation under the non-
smooth ALD likelihood — including mixing time bounds and the effect of the smoothing
parameterεon the approximation error in the acceptance ratio — remain to be established.
