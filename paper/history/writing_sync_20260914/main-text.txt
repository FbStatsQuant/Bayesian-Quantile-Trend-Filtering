                                            Abstract
       Quantile trend filtering estimates time-varying conditional quantiles through a
   check loss and an ℓ1 difference penalty, with efficient frequentist estimation available
   via ADMM (Brantley et al., 2020). We develop an adaptive, fully Bayesian extension of
   this estimator. Under an asymmetric Laplace likelihood and a global Laplace difference
   prior, the conditional posterior mode at unit likelihood scale and fixed regularization
   parameter coincides with the frequentist ADMM solution. Assigning priors to both pa-
   rameters allows the regularization strength and likelihood scale to be inferred jointly
   with the trend. A three-block Gibbs scheme combines their conjugate updates with
   a proximal Metropolis-adjusted Langevin (P-MALA) trend update (Pereyra, 2016),
   using ADMM to solve the required trend filtering subproblems. The hierarchy uses
   one global regularization parameter per quantile, without observation-level likelihood
   augmentation or separate random scales for the trend differences. In the constrained
   proximal formulation, a fixed banded system permits factorization reuse and linear
   work in the sample size for a fixed number of inner ADMM iterations.
       The framework extends to joint estimation of multiple quantiles under exact non-
   crossing constraints, enforced through box projections embedded in the ADMM prox-
   imal operator, with each quantile updated sequentially by its own P-MALA step. For
   extreme probability levels, where the near-constant gradient of the asymmetric Laplace
   likelihood causes gradient-based samplers to drift, we introduce a sentinel quantile
   strategy: auxiliary quantiles placed beyond the target range whose box constraints
   confine the target within a dynamically updating interval, preventing drift while pre-
   serving exploration.
       Simulation studies spanning piecewise constant, varying smoothness, and smooth
   signals under Gaussian, Beta, and mixed normal noise examine estimation accuracy
   at interior and extreme quantile levels, with posterior uncertainty summaries obtained
   from the sampled trends. The results show competitive estimation relative to the

1    Introduction
The estimation of a time-varying conditional quantile from a single observed time series
is a fundamental problem in nonparametric statistics, arising in environmental monitoring,
biomedical signal processing, finance, and astrophysics, among other fields. Unlike mean
regression, quantile estimation provides a direct view of the upper or lower tails of the
response distribution, which are often of greater scientific interest than the central tendency.
When the underlying trend is believed to be piecewise polynomial—constant level shifts,
piecewise linear behavior, or piecewise quadratic curvature—trend filtering provides a flexible
and computationally tractable framework, producing estimates that adapt automatically to
abrupt changes while remaining smooth in regions of slow variation.
     The frequentist approach to quantile trend filtering was developed by Brantley et al.
(2020), who combined the check loss of Koenker and Bassett Jr (1978) with the discrete
difference penalty of Tibshirani (2014) and solved the resulting nonsmooth convex program
via the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM). Their estimator scales well
to large signals and inherits the locally adaptive properties of ℓ1 trend filtering. Theoreti-
cal properties, including risk bounds, have been studied by Madrid Padilla and Chatterjee
(2022). This approach provides a natural benchmark for estimation, but its output is a
point estimate at a selected penalty level. Regularization selection, for example through an
extended Bayesian information criterion, and uncertainty quantification require procedures
beyond the ADMM optimization itself.
     We develop an adaptive, fully Bayesian extension of quantile trend filtering computed
by ADMM. The connection is exact: under an asymmetric Laplace likelihood and a global
Laplace prior on the trend differences, the conditional posterior mode at σ = 1 and fixed
λ coincides with the frequentist estimator. We extend this formulation by learning the
regularization strength and likelihood scale jointly with the trend, and use ADMM within
posterior sampling to retain its useful computational structure. This retains a posterior
distribution over the trend while avoiding the growing collection of latent-scale updates and
repeated matrix computations that make conventional augmented Gibbs implementations
costly for long time series. Section 2.2 establishes the mode equivalence.
     Related progress on Bayesian quantile regression in additive and spline settings has been
made by Fasiolo et al. (2021), though their approach targets smooth additive models rather
than piecewise polynomial trends of arbitrary order. The analogous trajectory for mean trend
filtering has been traced by Heng, Zhou, and Chi, who placed the ℓ1 trend filtering problem
of Tibshirani (2014) in a Bayesian framework using proximal Markov chain Monte Carlo
techniques (Pereyra, 2016). The quantile setting introduces an additional computational
difficulty: both the check loss and the difference penalty are nonsmooth. The challenge is
therefore to preserve the useful optimization structure while making joint posterior inference
computationally practical.
     The Bayesian quantile trend filtering method of Onizuka et al. (2024) illustrates this
computational challenge. Its Gibbs sampler uses latent-variable representations of the like-
lihood and shrinkage prior, requiring observation-level latent updates, local-scale updates
for the trend differences, and a Gaussian trend update with a precision matrix that changes
each iteration. These updates support flexible local shrinkage, especially under the horse-
shoe prior, but impose substantial computational overhead. In the supplied implementation,

                                               4
dense inversion and Gaussian simulation remain a bottleneck even with the tested sparse op-
tion enabled. Our formulation instead uses one global regularization parameter per quantile
and an ADMM proximal system that permits factorization reuse.
    The same authors also propose a faster mean-field variational Bayes (VB) approximation,
together with a calibration procedure for its uncertainty summaries (Onizuka et al., 2024). In
exploratory runs beyond the n = 100 reference setting, we encountered numerical failures in
the public VB implementation. This implementation-specific observation provides additional
motivation for a sampling alternative; our comparisons focus on estimation accuracy and
computational performance rather than a quantitative assessment of uncertainty calibration.
    The present paper combines the mode connection with a computational use of ADMM:
the same class of ℓ1 trend filtering subproblems becomes the engine of posterior simula-
tion. We construct a three-block Gibbs scheme that updates the single global regularization
parameter λ and the likelihood scale σ from conjugate full conditionals, and updates the
trend through a proximal Metropolis-adjusted Langevin (P-MALA) step (Pereyra, 2016).
This formulation retains the local adaptation induced by the ℓ1 difference penalty without
introducing a separate random shrinkage scale for every difference or a latent variable for
every observation. For each quantile, only two scalar hyperparameters are sampled in addi-
tion to the trend. The proximal subproblems are solved using ADMM. In the constrained
formulation, their coefficient matrix depends only on the difference operator and fixed algo-
rithmic parameters, permitting reuse of a banded factorization. With sparse operations and
a bounded number of inner ADMM iterations, the work per outer iteration is linear in the
sample size. The advantage is thus a simpler hierarchy and reusable optimization structure,
together with posterior inference for the trend and its regularization strength.
    The framework extends naturally to joint inference across multiple quantile levels under
the standard non-crossing constraint. The single-quantile P-MALA step is replaced by a
sequential update in which the proximal map is solved under box constraints determined
by the most recent values of the neighboring quantile trends. The required modification
to the ADMM solver introduces a single auxiliary variable and an elementwise projection
step, leaving its overall structure and computational profile intact. This joint construction,
however, also exposes a difficulty intrinsic to gradient-based samplers under the asymmetric
Laplace likelihood at extreme quantile levels. For τ close to zero or one, nearly all residuals
share the same sign and the gradient of the smoothed check loss approaches a constant vector,
so the P-MALA proposal carries negligible directional information and the chain develops
a persistent drift away from the data. The locally adaptive prior of Onizuka et al. (2024)
partially mitigates this pathology, and their sampler produces degraded but not catastrophic
estimates at extreme quantile levels. We instead resolve it through a strategy that exploits
the box-constraint structure of the joint sampler: we augment the set of inferential quantile
levels with auxiliary sentinel quantiles placed beyond the target range, whose role is purely
structural. The sentinels are sampled as ordinary members of the joint posterior and provide
a dynamically updating upper or lower bound on the extreme target quantile through the
non-crossing constraint, confining its exploration to a statistically sensible region at every
iteration. The mechanism is computationally cheap and, in our simulation study, allows
the proposed sampler to match or slightly improve upon the extreme-quantile accuracy of
Onizuka et al. (2024) while using a globally penalized prior that is structurally simpler than
the per-coefficient horseshoe.

                                              5
    The main contributions of this work are as follows.
    1. We develop an adaptive Bayesian extension of the frequentist quantile trend filtering
       estimator: its conditional posterior mode recovers the ADMM solution at σ = 1 and
       fixed λ, while the full hierarchy learns both parameters jointly with the trend and
       propagates their uncertainty into posterior summaries.
    2. We use ADMM within proximal posterior sampling, with one global regularization
       parameter per quantile and no observation-level likelihood augmentation. The con-
       strained proximal system permits reuse of a banded factorization; sparse operations
       give linear work in n for a fixed inner iteration budget, avoiding the dense inverse and
       Gaussian simulation used by the supplied Gibbs benchmark.
    3. We extend the sampler to the joint estimation of multiple quantile levels under non-
       crossing constraints, incorporating the constraints exactly through a box-projection
       step inside the inner ADMM solver rather than through approximate or penalty-based
       enforcement.
    4. We introduce the sentinel quantile strategy, a structurally minimal device that resolves
       the gradient degeneracy of proximal MALA at extreme quantile levels and recovers
       competitive accuracy at the tails of the response distribution. The mechanism is generic
       and applies to any gradient-based sampler for quantile trend filtering, not only the one
       developed here.
    5. Through a comprehensive simulation study spanning four signal structures, three noise
       distributions, three difference orders, and both interior and extreme quantile levels, we
       demonstrate that the proposed sampler delivers accuracy comparable to or better than
       the competitive Bayesian approach of Onizuka et al. (2024), with posterior uncertainty
       summaries and reduced computational overhead relative to the supplied dense Gibbs
       implementation, and that it does so using a single global penalty parameter rather than
       the per-coordinate local shrinkage employed by competing priors, which simplifies the
       prior specification and the resulting sampler.
   The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the methodology,
presenting the data model and likelihood, the individual-quantile sampler with its three
conjugate and proximal updates, the joint sampler with non-crossing constraints and the
associated ADMM proximal operator, and the sentinel quantile strategy. Section 3 reports
the simulation study comparing the proposed sampler with the relevant frequentist and
Bayesian competitors across interior and extreme quantile levels. Section 5 discusses the
results, limitations, and avenues for extension.

2.2    Individual Quantile Sampler
We first describe the sampler that targets a single quantile level τ . The joint sampler of
Section 2.3 builds directly on this construction.

Prior specification. To encourage smoothness of the estimated trend, we place a penalty
on the (k + 1)-th order discrete differences of θ. Let D = D(k+1) ∈ Rm×n denote the discrete
difference operator of order k+1, where m = n−k −1 is its row rank. We use the hierarchical
prior

                                 p(θ | λ) ∝ exp{−λ ∥Dθ∥1 } ,

which corresponds to placing independent Laplace distributions on the m differences (Dθ)j ,
all sharing the common rate parameter λ (Laplace scale 1/λ). Thus, the ℓ1 penalty retains
the local adaptation of trend filtering, while λ controls the overall regularization strength.

   Rather than treating λ as a fixed tuning parameter selected by an external criterion, we
place a Gamma hyperprior on λ,
                                     λ ∼ Gamma(a0 , b0 ),
with shape a0 > 0 and rate b0 > 0. For the ALD scale, we place an inverse-Gamma prior
                                        σ ∼ IG(aσ , bσ ),
with shape aσ > 0 and rate bσ > 0. In our implementation we set aσ = bσ = 0.1, a weakly
informative choice that lets the data dominate the posterior for σ. Both hyperpriors are
conjugate given θ, yielding closed-form full conditionals that are sampled as part of the
inference.

Hyperparameter scaling by difference order. The conjugate full conditional for λ
is Gamma(a0 + m, b0 + SD ) with posterior mean (a0 + m)/(b0 + SD ), where SD = ∥Dθ∥1 .
Because m = n−k−1 grows linearly with n, the appropriate scaling of (a0 , b0 ) depends on the
difference order k, and the qualitative consequences of misspecification differ markedly across
orders. The guiding principle is that the posterior mean of λ should scale appropriately with
the complexity of the signal class being fit.
    For k = 0, the estimator is piecewise constant and an unbounded posterior mean of λ
obliterates the jump structure of the signal by shrinking the first differences of θ toward zero.
The required scaling is a0 = c1 n and b0 = n, yielding posterior mean approximately c1 + 1
independent of n, with prior variance c1 /n that shrinks with sample size. We use c1 = 1 as
the default and recommend the posterior mode as the point estimate. Alternative scalings
of the form a0 = 1, b0 = c2 nα with α < 1 produce a posterior mean of order n1−α , which
diverges and is not viable for piecewise constant signals.
    For k = 1, the estimator is piecewise linear, and slow growth of λ with n is appropriate:
more data warrant stronger regularization, but excessive penalization suppresses√local fea-
tures such as spikes or sharp curvature
                                √          transitions. We set a0 = 1 and b0 = c2 n, giving
posterior mean approximately n/c2 . The shape parameter a0 = 1 is nearly noninformative,
so the posterior mean of λ is driven by m and the sufficient statistic SD . We use c2 = 1 as
the default, with the range [0.5, 2] providing a reasonable operating window, and recommend
the posterior mean as the point estimate.                                               ⃦      ⃦
    For k = 2, the estimator is piecewise quadratic and the sufficient statistic SD = ⃦D(3) θ⃦1

point estimate. This order exhibits a degree of robustness that lower orders do not share:
misspecification of c produces gradual degradation in mean squared error rather than the
catastrophic structural collapse observed for k = 0.
    We adopt k = 2 as the default difference order in the absence of prior knowledge about
signal structure. It does not require commitment to a specific signal class, exhibits the
lowest sensitivity to hyperparameter misspecification among the three orders, and degrades
gracefully under it. Lower orders may outperform k = 2 when the signal class matches the
estimator structure exactly and the hyperparameters are well-tuned, but the gain is modest
relative to the additional tuning effort required.

2.5    Sentinel Quantile Strategy for Extreme Quantile Estimation
A fundamental difficulty in Bayesian sampling for extreme quantile trend filtering arises
from the behavior of the ALD likelihood gradient. For a quantile level τ close to zero or
one, nearly all residuals yi − θi have the same sign, and the gradient of the smoothed check
loss ∇f˜(θ) approaches a constant vector. The P-MALA proposal then carries negligible
gradient information and degenerates toward a random walk. Because the posterior at
extreme quantile levels has a heavy tail in the direction away from the bulk of the data, the
Markov chain occasionally accepts large moves in that direction, causing persistent upward
or downward drift. For the 0.99 quantile with n = 400, this pathology produces large RMSE
values under individual estimation, rendering the estimate essentially useless. Onizuka et al.
(2024) document degraded coverage at τ ∈ {0.05, 0.95} across their full simulation suite,
which spans two signal structures—piecewise constant (PC) and varying smoothness (VS)—
under three noise distributions: Gaussian, Beta, and mixed normal.
    The sentinel quantile strategy resolves this by exploiting the sequential box constraint
structure of the joint sampler. We augment the set of quantiles of inferential interest with
auxiliary sentinel quantiles placed beyond the target range. The sentinels are treated as full
members of the joint posterior: they are sampled by the same Gibbs scheme, receive their
own regularization and scale parameters, and their estimates are computable. However they
are not primary targets of inference; their role is structural.
    Concretely, to estimate the 0.99 quantile stably, we include sentinel quantiles at 0.995
and 0.999. The 0.995 sentinel provides a dynamic upper wall for the 0.99 quantile through
the box constraint θ0.99,i ≤ θ0.995,i , and is itself controlled from above by the 0.999 sentinel
through θ0.995,i ≤ θ0.999,i . A stable scaffold of lower quantiles (e.g., 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95,
0.97, 0.98) anchors the structure from below, preventing downward drift of the 0.99 quantile.
The result is that the 0.99 quantile is sandwiched within a dynamically updating interval at


                                                13
every Gibbs iteration, which confines its exploration to a statistically sensible region without
imposing any fixed constraint on its value. The strategy is computationally free in the sense
that adding sentinel quantiles increases the number of sequential Gibbs steps by the number
of sentinels, which is small, without changing the per-step cost.

a single Laplace prior on Dθ with one shared scale λ, and calibrates the effective noise level
through a single scale σ; both parameters admit closed-form conjugate full conditionals—
Gamma for λ, inverse-Gamma
                   ∑︁              for σ—driven respectively by the sufficient statistics SD =
∥Dθ∥1 and Sρ = i ρτ (yi −θi ), so that the amount of smoothing and the assumed noise scale
both self-adjust to the current fit of θ without any external tuning step. This is a substantially
thinner hierarchy than the horseshoe construction of Onizuka (Horseshoe), which places a
local shrinkage parameter on every one of the n coordinates of Dθ in addition to a global
scale, and it is this added hierarchy, not a fundamentally different fitting principle, that gives
the horseshoe its local adaptivity to jumps and its edge on the piecewise constant signal.
The simulation results are consistent with this trade-off. Against the Laplace variant, which
shares the individual sampler’s single-scale prior but fixes λ by an external criterion rather
than adapting it within the chain, the individual sampler is on par or ahead at essentially
every level, signal, and noise distribution, indicating that the conjugate self-tuning of λ
and σ is doing real work. Against the horseshoe, the individual sampler trails by small
absolute margins on the piecewise constant signal, where isolated jumps reward coordinate-
specific shrinkage; is closely competitive on the varying smoothness signal; and surpasses the
horseshoe at nearly every level on the smooth signal, whose complete results are reported
in Appendix A.1.2. In no configuration does either method fail to recover the signal: the
comparison is one of fine-grained ordering among estimators that all fit well, not of success
against failure. Because the horseshoe’s advantage is concentrated in signals with strong local
heterogeneity, and because it is bought with n additional per-coordinate parameters sampled
at every Gibbs iteration against the individual sampler’s two, the individual sampler offers a
favorable accuracy-to-complexity trade-off: it recovers most of the horseshoe’s benefit where
that benefit exists, exceeds it where it does not, and does so with a model that is easier to
specify, easier to diagnose, and cheaper to update per iteration.

Default difference order. When the smoothness of the underlying trend is not known
in advance, our default recommendation is the difference order k = 2. A penalty on second
differences fits piecewise linear trends and thereby provides a reasonable local approximation
to a wide variety of quantile functions, from globally smooth curves to signals with localized
features, degrading gracefully when the truth departs from the assumed order. The lower
orders are more specialized: k = 0 is well matched only when the trend is genuinely piecewise
constant, while k = 1 occupies an intermediate position. Accordingly, the order-specific fits
reported in this section and Appendix A should be read as each order evaluated on its
best-case signal, with k = 2 the sensible choice absent prior structural knowledge.

4     Data Applications
4.1    Air Quality Sensor Data
Data. Our first application revisits the fenceline air-quality monitoring problem that mo-
tivated quantile trend filtering in Brantley et al. (2020). The U.S. Environmental Protection
Agency deploys low-cost SPod sensors on the perimeter of industrial facilities to detect fugi-
tive emissions of volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Each SPod records, once per second,
the raw signal of a photoionization detector (PID): a UV lamp ionizes VOC molecules in
the sampled air and the resulting current, reported in uncalibrated counts, is proportional
to the total concentration of ionizable compounds. The series has three components with
sharply different time scales. Emission plumes crossing the sensor produce large one-sided
spikes lasting seconds to a few minutes; the electronic baseline drifts slowly over hours with
temperature, humidity, and lamp condition; and high-frequency instrument noise rides on
top of both. The scientific interest lies exclusively in the spikes, but because each unit’s
baseline drifts idiosyncratically and the counts are not calibrated across units, the drifting
baseline must be estimated and removed before events can be flagged or compared across
sensors. Since the plumes are strictly one-sided excursions above the baseline, the baseline
is identified by a low conditional quantile of the series, making this a natural quantile trend
filtering problem in which, in contrast to the usual convention, the trend is the nuisance and
the residual carries the signal. We analyze one day of data, June 15, 2018, from sensor d
of the two collocated SPods in the EPA Rubbertown dataset accompanying Brantley et al.
(2020), aggregating the 86,400 raw 1-Hz observations into 10-second block means, which
yields n = 8,640 observations and attenuates the strong serial correlation of the 1-Hz noise
that the ALD working likelihood does not model.

Model and settings. We estimate the baseline at the three levels τ ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1}
with k = 2, so the prior favours piecewise quadratic trends, each level fitted independently
by the individual sampler of Section 2.2 on the standardized series. The sampler is run
for 6,000 Gibbs iterations with a burn-in of 1,000, proximal parameter γ = 0.1, smoothing
parameter ϵ = 0.01, priors λ ∼ Gamma(1, 10) and σ ∼ IG(0.01, 0.01), and a step size
adapted during burn-in toward acceptance rate 0.5. Because SD (θ) is small for the smooth
baselines supported by these data, the rate parameter of the Gamma prior effectively pins
the posterior of λ near (n − k − 1)/10 ≈ 864, so the prior acts as a smoothness dial; we
return to this point below. As the frequentist benchmark we use the ADMM solver of the
detrendr package of Brantley et al. (2020) with the smoothing parameter selected by eBIC
over the grid λ ∈ exp{−2, . . . , 12}, which selects λ = 54.6 at τ = 0.01 and λ = 1096.6 at


                                              23
τ ∈ {0.05, 0.1}. Following Brantley et al. (2020), event detection after baseline removal is a
threshold rule on the detrended series y − θ:ˆ︁ a point is classified as signal when it exceeds
the empirical 90th, 95th, or 99th percentile of the detrended data themselves, so that no
absolute calibration of the counts is required.

Results. Figure 1 overlays the three fitted baselines on the data for the proposed sam-
pler; the corresponding ADMM fits, and the detrended series they produce, are reported in
Appendix B.1. Both methods track the overnight rise, the large morning excursion around
05:00, and the slow afternoon decline, and both leave the plume spikes untouched above
the fit. The empirical calibration of the posterior median baselines is close to nominal: the

resulting ADMM curve bends upward into the 05:00 plume and crosses the τ = 0.05 and

prior at all three levels, remain ordered and essentially parallel throughout the day. Figure 2

fewer than one observation in a hundred lies below the trend and the smoothed check-loss

Carlo error at this chain length.

Data. The Munich rent data come from the 2003 Munich rental guide (Mietspiegel ), a

We use the N = 2,035 apartments in the version distributed with the spmrf package. The
response y is the net rent per square metre (EUR per month) and the covariate x is the floor

Model and settings. Both features are accommodated without changing the samplers.
The trend θ ∈ Rn is indexed by the 134 distinct sizes, and the ALD likelihood sums the check
loss over all apartments sharing a size, so that each θj is informed by every rent recorded

equally spaced. We fit k = 1, so the prior favours piecewise linear trends in floor size, at the

acceptance rates between 0.42 and 0.61 across the five levels. As the point of comparison

own code, which handles repeated observations and irregular grids natively, with the same
k, the same quantile levels, and the same number of iterations and retained draws. For both
methods the point estimate is the posterior mean of θ and the bands are 95% pointwise
credible intervals computed from the 5,000 retained draws.

the globally penalized P-MALA approach is most pronounced on the smooth signal, where
the global prior’s implicit bias toward a homogeneous degree of smoothness is well matched
to the truth and the sampler attains the lowest errors at nearly every interior level. The
ordering reverses, as expected, on the piecewise constant signal under Gaussian and mixed
normal noise at interior quantiles, where the per-coefficient horseshoe prior’s local adaptivity
gives it the edge. The margins in that setting are small in absolute terms—all three methods
recover the signal accurately—but their direction is systematic, consistent with the general
principle that local adaptivity benefits estimation when the target signal has genuinely het-
erogeneous smoothness, with the associated computational and modeling complexity being
the price paid for that benefit.

Scalability. A central motivation for the proposed approach is to make Bayesian inference
practical while retaining the optimization structure of frequentist quantile trend filtering.
The supplied Gibbs implementation of Onizuka et al. (2024) forms a dense inverse and
uses dense Gaussian simulation at each iteration. Our audit found that its sparse option
still enters this dense computational path in the benchmark environment, so enabling that
option does not remove the principal bottleneck. The augmented hierarchy also updates
observation-level latent variables and local prior scales, whereas our formulation samples
one global regularization parameter and one likelihood scale per quantile in addition to the
trend.


                                                                  29
    This distinction should be separated from an asymptotic claim about all Gibbs implemen-
tations. At fixed difference order, a Gaussian draw based directly on the banded precision
can be computed in O(n) work without forming an inverse, although the changing weights
generally require a new numerical factorization each iteration. For our constrained proxi-
mal system, A = γ −1 I + ρD⊤ D + ρI is independent of the sampled parameters, allowing
factor reuse when D, γ, and ρ remain fixed. Sparse operations give O(nJ) work for J inner
ADMM sweeps, plus linear setup and sufficient-statistic calculations. Practical comparisons
must account for the inner iteration count, factorization reuse in the actual implementation,
and mixing of the complete chain. Timing per outer iteration alone does not establish cost
per effective posterior draw.

Role of the scale parameter. Treating the ALD scale σ as an unknown parameter rather
than fixing it at the optimization convention of unity is a substantive modeling decision with
several consequences. Within the Gibbs scheme, σ and λ interact with the trend estimate
in complementary ways: σ calibrates the effective likelihood temperature, controlling how
sharply the gradient of the smoothed check loss responds to residuals, while λ calibrates the
effective smoothness penalty. Decoupling these two sources of regularization allows each to
adapt to a different aspect of the data — noise level and signal roughness respectively — and
prevents the single tuning parameter of the frequentist formulation from having to absorb
both roles simultaneously. Learning σ allows the likelihood scale to respond to the data, but
does not by itself guarantee frequentist coverage when the asymmetric Laplace likelihood is
misspecified. Posterior sampling avoids the additional mean-field approximation used by VB;
coverage remains a separate question, as recognized by the calibration approach of Onizuka
et al. (2024). A practical caveat is that σ and λ are not fully separable in their effects on
the posterior for θ: both appear in the effective curvature seen by the P-MALA proposal,
and under severe misspecification their joint posterior may exhibit a soft ridge. The weakly
informative prior σ ∼ IG(0.1, 0.1) is sufficient to regularize this in all settings considered,
but sensitivity of the posterior to this prior choice warrants attention in applications where
the noise distribution is far from symmetric or unimodal.

Limitations. The globally penalized prior is the principal structural limitation of the
proposed approach. By placing a single shared penalty λ on all entries of Dθ, the model
encodes a homogeneous prior belief about the smoothness of the trend. This is appropriate
when the signal is globally smooth or when the difference order k is chosen to match the signal
class, as in the varying smoothness experiments, but it is a deliberate simplification relative
to the per-coefficient shrinkage of the horseshoe prior. On piecewise constant signals, the
globally penalized model must use a single λ to accommodate both flat segments and jump
locations, a bias-variance tradeoff that the horseshoe prior resolves more cleanly through
local adaptivity. The edge of Onizuka (Horseshoe) on the PC signal under Gaussian and
mixed normal noise is a direct consequence of this structural difference: it is systematic and
should not be expected to vanish with larger samples or better tuning, though in the settings
considered here it amounts to small absolute differences between estimators that both recover
the signal well. Users whose signals are known to be piecewise constant, and for whom these
margins matter, may prefer the locally adaptive prior at its higher computational cost.


                                              30
The sentinel strategy as a heuristic. The sentinel quantile construction introduced
in Section 2.5 resolves the gradient degeneracy of P-MALA at extreme quantile levels and
produces stable estimates in the simulation study. It is, however, a practical device rather
than a formally justified procedure. The specific recommendations — up to five sentinels per
side, equally spaced within each side, with spacings chosen to fill the available probability
room — are motivated by empirical experience and by the requirement that the sentinels
be close enough to the target to provide a tight dynamic bound while remaining far enough
apart to avoid degeneracy among themselves.

Extensions. Several directions for extension follow naturally from the present work. The
P-MALA framework accommodates non-Euclidean preconditioning through the proximal
operator, and a preconditioned variant exploiting the banded structure of D⊤ D may improve
mixing at higher difference orders without increasing per-iteration cost. The sentinel strategy
is applicable to any gradient-based sampler for quantile estimation and could be combined
with locally adaptive priors, potentially recovering the accuracy advantages of the horseshoe
prior at extreme quantile levels while retaining the computational structure of the present
approach. Extension to multivariate or spatially indexed signals requires generalization of
the difference operator D to non-chain graph structures, for which the banded linear system
no longer applies directly; spectral or multigrid solvers may serve a similar role in those
settings. Finally, the theoretical properties of the P-MALA approximation under the non-
smooth ALD likelihood — including mixing time bounds and the effect of the smoothing
parameter ε on the approximation error in the acceptance ratio — remain to be established.

At the extreme levels the Onizuka variants are more competitive on the smooth signal than
at the interior levels. The horseshoe has the lowest MSE under Gaussian and mixed normal

distributions, reflecting the sentinel-induced geometric confinement discussed in Section 3.2.
