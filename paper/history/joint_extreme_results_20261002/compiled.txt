                                            Abstract
       Quantile trend filtering combines the check loss with an ℓ1 penalty on discrete dif-
   ferences of the trend. We develop Bayesian-PMALA, a posterior sampler for quantile
   trend filtering that learns the regularization strength and the likelihood scale jointly
   with the trend. Augmenting the asymmetric Laplace likelihood with its normal–
   exponential representation makes the likelihood Gaussian in the trend. Under a global
   Laplace prior on trend differences, the whole negative log-conditional of the trend
   is then a weighted quadratic plus an ℓ1 difference penalty. The trend can therefore
   be updated by an exact proximal Metropolis-adjusted Langevin (P-MALA) step. The
   Moreau–Yosida envelope is built on the entire conditional posterior, through a weighted
   trend filtering problem, rather than on a smoothed check loss, and the Metropolis–
   Hastings correction uses the exact density. The latent variables, the penalty and the
   scale have conjugate updates. Every linear system is banded, and the proximal map
   is computed by ADMM with a factorization shared by the forward and reverse maps,
   so the work per sweep grows linearly with the sample size for a fixed inner iteration
   budget. For several quantile levels, a joint version samples each trend between its
   neighbours. Only the proximal map changes, becoming a box-constrained problem
   solved with banded interior-point steps, and no retained draw crosses. One default
   configuration is used for every signal, noise distribution and quantile level. For series
   contaminated by transient bursts, a stronger prior on the penalty is fixed before the
   data are seen.
       In simulations with varying-smoothness and smooth signals under Gaussian, Beta,
   and mixed-normal noise, Bayesian-PMALA attains accuracy comparable to a locally
   adaptive horseshoe Gibbs sampler with a simpler hierarchy and banded computations,
   and accuracy comparable to ADMM-based quantile trend filtering while providing
   posterior inference on the trend, the penalty and the scale. In a peak-detection design
   for air-quality sensors, it recovers the peak-free baseline far more accurately than the
   horseshoe sampler and comes close to the ADMM benchmark in baseline recovery

1     Introduction
Time-varying conditional quantiles describe how the lower tail, center, and upper tail of a
response evolve across an ordered domain. They are useful when changes in variability or
asymmetry matter as much as changes in location, as in environmental monitoring, growth
assessment, and economic time series. Quantile trend filtering combines the check loss of
Koenker and Bassett (1978) with an ℓ1 penalty on discrete differences, adapting a piecewise
polynomial fit to local changes in the signal (Tibshirani, 2014; Madrid Padilla and Chatterjee,
2022).
     Brantley et al. (2020) developed a quantile trend filtering approach for smoothly varying
baseline drift, including joint non-crossing estimation, smoothing-parameter selection, and
an alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) algorithm. Their formulation pro-
vides an effective optimization-based benchmark, especially for the smooth-baseline setting
that motivates the method. At a selected regularization level, however, the optimizer sup-
plies point estimates; posterior simulation requires a probabilistic model and an inference
procedure beyond that optimization step.
     We develop a Bayesian extension that retains this computational connection. Under an
asymmetric Laplace working likelihood and a global Laplace prior on trend differences, the
conditional posterior mode at fixed regularization parameter λ and likelihood scale σ is the
quantile trend filtering solution with penalty λσ. We infer λ and σ jointly with the trend
instead of selecting the penalty by an external criterion. The posterior mean need not equal
the optimizer, but both arise from the same check-loss and difference-penalty structure, and
Section 2.1 states the connection explicitly.
     Related methods include Bayesian and calibrated loss-based quantile smoothing. Fasi-
olo et al. (2021) developed fast calibrated additive quantile regression. For quantile trend
filtering, Onizuka et al. (2024) combined an asymmetric Laplace likelihood with shrinkage

                                               4
priors and developed both Gibbs sampling and calibrated variational approximations. Their
horseshoe prior supports coefficient-specific shrinkage, allowing the amount of regularization
to vary across the trend. The supplied Gibbs implementation uses latent-variable and local-
scale updates together with a changing Gaussian precision matrix; its matrix operations
motivate an alternative computational route for long signals.
    Proximal Markov chain Monte Carlo provides such a route for nonsmooth models. The
proximal Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (P-MALA) of Pereyra (2016) replaces the
nonsmooth negative log-density by its Moreau–Yosida envelope, whose gradient is given by
a proximal map, proposes a Langevin step on the envelope, and corrects with the exact
density through a Metropolis–Hastings step. Its requirement is the proximal map of the
whole negative log-density. Related schemes split the density into a smooth part handled by
its gradient and a nonsmooth part handled by a proximal map (Durmus et al., 2018), and
in mean trend filtering Heng et al. (2023) used proximal Bayesian computation to learn the
trend, scale, and regularization parameters. Quantile trend filtering is harder because the
likelihood is nonsmooth as well as the prior. The check loss has no gradient, so a splitting
scheme must smooth it, which changes the target that the proposal approximates. Combined
with the difference penalty it does not give a trend filtering problem whose proximal map
is routinely computable. Being piecewise linear, it also carries no curvature with which to
precondition a proposal.
    We resolve this with the normal–exponential representation of the asymmetric Laplace
distribution (Kozumi and Kobayashi, 2011). Conditionally on one latent variable per obser-
vation, the likelihood is Gaussian in the trend. Integrating the latent variables out returns
the asymmetric Laplace likelihood exactly, so the augmentation changes the sampler and not
the model. Under a global Laplace prior on the differences, the negative log full conditional
of the trend is then a weighted quadratic plus the ℓ1 difference penalty. Its proximal map is
a weighted trend filtering problem, which we solve by ADMM with a banded factorization.
The augmentation is what allows the trend to be updated by P-MALA in its exact form, as
Pereyra (2016) define it, on the full conditional posterior. The Moreau–Yosida envelope is
built on the entire negative log-conditional, likelihood and prior together, and its gradient
costs one proximal map. The Langevin proposal is a Gaussian centred at that proximal
point, so its density is explicit. The Metropolis–Hastings step is evaluated with the exact
conditional density. No part of the check loss is smoothed and the target is not split, so the
chain leaves the stated posterior invariant. The proximal map enters the kernel only as a
deterministic function of its input, so solving it with a fixed ADMM budget affects the effi-
ciency of the proposal, not the posterior the chain targets. The augmentation also supplies
the curvature that the check loss lacks: the precision of the augmented likelihood, together
with a difference term on the scale of the prior, defines the metric that preconditions the
proposal.
    The resulting Gibbs sampler, which we call Bayesian-PMALA, pairs this P-MALA update
of the trend with conjugate updates for the latent variables, the global penalty λ and the scale
σ, and with an ancillarity–sufficiency interweaving step (Yu and Meng, 2011) that improves
the mixing of λ. The hierarchy has one global penalty per quantile rather than a vector of
local shrinkage scales. Every linear system is banded, and one factorization serves both the
forward and the reverse proximal maps of each move, so for a fixed ADMM iteration budget
the work per sweep grows linearly with the sample size. A single default configuration, with

                                               5
difference order k = 2 and fixed hyperparameters, is used for every signal, noise distribution
and quantile level.
    The same construction extends to several quantile levels whose curves must not cross.
Brantley et al. (2020) impose non-crossing in the optimization; we impose it on the posterior,
as an ordering constraint on the trends in a joint target formed from the single-level poste-
riors. Given its neighbours, the trend at each level has its single-level conditional restricted
to a box. The negative log-density of that conditional is still convex, so P-MALA applies
unchanged. Only the proximal map changes, and it becomes a box-constrained weighted
trend filtering problem. A proposal outside the box is rejected, so the Metropolis–Hastings
correction keeps its single-level form and needs no truncation constant. The constrained
map is computed by the unconstrained ADMM when that solution respects the bounds, and
otherwise by a primal–dual interior-point method whose Newton systems are banded, so the
work per map remains linear in n. The latent-variable, penalty and scale updates are those
of the single-level sampler, the interweaving step is restricted to the box, and every retained
draw is ordered at the observed indices.
    Our simulation study focuses on two established benchmarks: the varying-smoothness
signal used by Onizuka et al. (2024) and the sinusoidal smooth signal used by Brantley et al.
(2020), under Gaussian, Beta, and mixed-normal noise with the noise parameterization of
Onizuka et al. We compare Bayesian-PMALA with the two Onizuka MCMC variants and
the ADMM point estimator at quantile levels from 0.05 to 0.95, on identical data sets. Each
competitor is most accurate on the benchmark that suits it, the horseshoe on the varying-
smoothness signal and ADMM on the smooth signal, while Bayesian-PMALA is competitive
on both: it attains accuracy comparable to the locally adaptive horseshoe sampler with
a simpler hierarchy and banded computations, and accuracy comparable to ADMM while
providing posterior inference on the trend, the penalty and the likelihood scale. A third
experiment adopts the peak-detection design of Brantley et al. (2020), where the target
is a peak-free low-quantile baseline of a contaminated series and estimation is followed by
a classification step. Because the likelihood cannot separate a burst from a movement of
the trend, this setting uses a stronger prior on the penalty, fixed in advance, with the same
sampler; Bayesian-PMALA then recovers the baseline far more accurately than the horseshoe
sampler and comes close to ADMM in both baseline recovery and detection.
    Bayesian-PMALA thus sits between computationally efficient optimization and a more
elaborate locally adaptive Bayesian hierarchy. Relative to ADMM, it adds exact posterior
sampling and learns the regularization and likelihood scales instead of selecting a penalty
by an information criterion. Relative to the Onizuka Gibbs sampler, it replaces a vector
of local shrinkage scales with one global penalty per quantile and replaces dense matrix
computations with banded proximal computations; per sweep it runs 25 to 7,000 times
faster than the supplied implementation for n between 250 and 4000 (Section 4). Like the
joint ADMM formulation, its joint version prevents crossing, here on every posterior draw.
    Section 2 presents the model, Bayesian-PMALA and its joint non-crossing extension.
Appendix A gives the computational details, including how the unconstrained and the box-
constrained proximal problems are solved. Section 3 reports the simulation study, with
additional tables in Appendix B. Section 6 presents the data applications, and Section 7
discusses the findings and limitations.

2       Methodology
Section 2.1 sets out the model for a single quantile level, its augmented posterior and the
Gibbs sampler that explores it, in which the trend is updated by a proximal Metropolis-
adjusted Langevin (P-MALA) step. Section 2.2 extends the sampler to several quantile levels
whose curves are required not to cross. The extension keeps the model and every conditional
update of the single-level sampler except one: the trend is now sampled from a conditional
distribution restricted to a box, so its proximal map becomes a constrained optimization
problem. How the two proximal problems are solved is described in Appendices A.2 and
A.3.

2.1     Bayesian quantile trend filtering at a single level
Let y = (y1 , . . . , yn )⊤ ∈ Rn denote an observed series, and model

                                 yi = θi + εi ,       i = 1, . . . , n,                        (1)

where θ = (θ1 , . . . , θn )⊤ is an unknown trend and the errors εi are independent. For a quantile
level τ ∈ (0, 1) the target is the trend θ whose i-th entry is the conditional τ -quantile of yi .

2.1.1    Prior distributions
Let D = D(k+1) ∈ Rm×n be the discrete difference operator of order k + 1, with m = n − k − 1
rows. We place independent Laplace distributions with common rate λ on the m differences
(Dθ)j ,
                            p(θ | λ) ∝ λm exp{−λ ∥Dθ∥1 } .                               (2)
The prior is flat on the (k +1)-dimensional null space of D, the polynomials of degree at most
k, a component that the likelihood identifies. The ℓ1 penalty retains the local adaptivity of
trend filtering (Tibshirani, 2014), and the single global rate λ controls the overall amount of
smoothing. The rate and the scale σ of the likelihood below receive conjugate priors,

                            λ ∼ Gamma(a0 , b0 ),         σ ∼ IG(aσ , bσ ),                     (3)

in the shape and rate parameterizations.

Default difference order and hyperparameters. Following Brantley et al. (2020), we
use k = 2 throughout, so the fitted trends are piecewise quadratic. The default hyperpa-
rameters are a0 = 1, b0 = 10−3 and aσ = bσ = 0.1. They are fixed before any data are seen
and are used for every signal, noise distribution and quantile level reported in Section 3; no
hyperparameter is selected by an external criterion, cross-validation or a search over a grid
of λ values. The small rate b0 keeps the prior on λ weak over the range of penalties relevant
to trend filtering, and the inverse-Gamma prior on σ is proper but carries negligible weight
against the n observations; Appendix A.9 gives the reasons for these choices. For series that
combine a smooth baseline with transient bursts, the prior on λ is replaced by the stronger
prior (33) of Section 5.2, with the sampler unchanged.

conditional (12), likelihood and penalty together, rather than smoothing one part and han-

Computation. Because W is diagonal and D⊤ D is banded, (13) is a weighted trend

its input (Appendix A.2). Every linear system involved is banded with half-bandwidth k + 1:
the ADMM system is factorized once per move and shared by the forward map T (θ) and the
reverse map T (θ⋆ ), M is factorized once to supply the noise L−⊤ ξ, and the two norms in (17)

per sweep therefore grows linearly with n. The step size δ is adapted toward an acceptance
rate of 0.5 (Appendix A.6).

4    Computational cost
This section measures how the running time of Bayesian-PMALA grows with the sample size
and compares it with the Laplace and horseshoe Gibbs samplers of Onizuka et al. (2024).
Both methods draw the trend from an asymmetric-Laplace working likelihood with the same
normal–exponential augmentation, and they differ in how the trend is updated: Bayesian-
PMALA by a P-MALA move built from banded linear algebra (Section 2.1.5), the Onizuka
samplers by an exact draw from a Gaussian full conditional. The comparison is of running
time, per Gibbs sweep and per run, for the two samplers as implemented.

What a sweep does. The trend update of Bayesian-PMALA computes the forward and
reverse proximal maps by ADMM with a fixed budget of 150 iterations each and a cold
start, on one banded Cholesky factor of the ADMM system shared by both maps, and
draws the proposal noise from one banded Cholesky factor of the metric M (Appendix A.2);
the latent-variable, penalty, interweaving and scale updates act elementwise. Because the
iteration budget is fixed and there is no data-dependent stopping rule, the work of a sweep
does not depend on the state of the chain.
    A sweep of the supplied Onizuka code draws θ ∼ N (Ab, σ 2 A) with A = (D⊤ Λ−1 D+C)−1 ,
where Λ holds the local scales and C the latent-variable weights, and then updates σ 2 , the
n latent variables and the local and global shrinkage parameters. With its sparse = TRUE
option, which we use throughout this section, D is stored as a sparse matrix and the precision
is assembled by sparse products; R’s solve then converts it to a dense matrix and forms
the full inverse A, and MASS::mvrnorm computes a complete eigendecomposition of σ 2 A and
forms the draw from the eigenvectors. The default sparse = FALSE, used in Section 3,
performs the same steps and assembles the precision by dense matrix products in addition.

Protocol. We use the varying-smoothness signal (27) with heteroscedastic Gaussian noise,
τ = 0.5, k = 2, and n ∈ {250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000}; at each (n, replicate) all three sam-
plers receive the same data set. Bayesian-PMALA runs with the default configuration of
Section 2.1. The Onizuka samplers are the code supplied with that paper, unmodified, with
sparse = TRUE, the IG(1, 1) prior on σ 2 of Section 3, and the packages that code attaches;
attaching the Matrix package as well would make solve dispatch to a sparse method, under
which the supplied code fails. Both samplers are timed with clock reads inserted at the
boundaries of their updates and no other change. All runs were made on one laptop (Intel
Core Ultra 7 155U, 32 GB memory, Windows 11, mains power) in R 4.4.1 with its single-
threaded reference BLAS, one configuration at a time, each in a fresh R process pinned to
one performance core at high priority. Bayesian-PMALA runs 600 sweeps; the first 300,
which comprise the 200 optimization warm-up sweeps of Appendix A.5 and 100 sweeps of
step-size adaptation, are excluded, and the reported time is the mean over the remaining
300, with acceptance rates of 0.40–0.49 in that window. An Onizuka sweep costs too much

after 2 at n = 1000, 3 after 1 at n = 2000, and 2 after 1 at n = 4000. Its time per sweep is
dominated by dense matrix computations whose work does not depend on the chain state,
so short windows suffice. Each entry is the median over replicates with distinct data seeds,

Scope. The difference in running time belongs to the supplied implementation, not to the

so the Gaussian draw could be obtained from a banded Cholesky factorization. Even a dense

sition, took 1.1 s at n = 2000 in the profile above, against 32 s for the three steps of the
supplied code. The comparison here is between Bayesian-PMALA and the implementation
used in Section 3, run in the setting that does less work. It concerns running time per sweep
and per run, on one data set, one quantile level and one difference order; the efficiency of
the chains per sweep is a separate question.

5.2    Fitting procedures and numerical settings
All methods use trend-filtering order k = 2, corresponding to a third-difference penalty and
piecewise-quadratic fitted trends. Bayesian-PMALA is the sampler of Section 2.1 with the
configuration used in Section 3: σ ∼ IG(0.1, 0.1), a chain started at θ = y, 30000 sweeps
of which the first 10000 are discarded, and the numerical settings of Appendix A. The only
difference is the prior on λ.
    The data model of this experiment, yi = bi +si +ei , differs from yi = θi +εi of Section 3 in a
way that the likelihood cannot resolve. The target is the baseline beneath the bursts, and the
asymmetric Laplace likelihood at a low quantile level does not distinguish a burst from a rapid
movement of the trend. Under the weak prior Gamma(1, 10−3 ) the posterior accommodates
the bursts in θ: the effective penalty λσ settles well below the penalty that extended BIC
selects for the same fits, and the posterior mean follows the peaks. The separation between
a smooth background and transient excursions is a structural assumption about the data,
and in a Bayesian analysis it enters through the prior. For series with bursts we therefore
use
                         λ ∼ Gamma(m, m/λ∗ ) ,          m = n − k − 1,                        (33)
with the same sampler. The prior mean is the constant λ∗ , which does not depend on n or
on the data. The shape m gives the prior the same weight as the m difference terms in the
conditional update λ | θ ∼ Gamma(a + m, b + ∥D(k+1) θ∥1 ), so the penalty remains random,
with posterior coefficient of variation near (2m)−1/2 . We set λ∗ = 5000 at the extreme level
τ = 0.01 and λ∗ = 30000 at the inner levels τ ∈ {0.05, 0.10}. The extreme level requires the
smaller value because a penalty that is too strong for the few observations below the curve
inflates σ, which raises the effective penalty λσ further until the trend move stops accepting.
Each constant is the largest value in a screening grid for which no chain stopped moving
at any n ∈ {500, . . . , 4000}; the screening used replications 1–3 at every sample size and
replications 1–20 at n = 1000. Because λ has the units of 1/y, the constants refer to the
scale of this design, whose noise standard deviation is 0.25; Section 6.1 applies the prior to
data on another scale.

6     Data Applications
6.1    Air Quality Sensor Data
Data. Our first application revisits the fenceline air-quality monitoring problem that mo-
tivated quantile trend filtering in Brantley et al. (2020). The U.S. Environmental Protection
Agency deploys low-cost SPod sensors on the perimeter of industrial facilities to detect fugi-
tive emissions of volatile organic compounds (VOCs). Each SPod records, once per second,
the raw signal of a photoionization detector (PID): a UV lamp ionizes VOC molecules in
the sampled air and the resulting current, reported in uncalibrated counts, is proportional
to the total concentration of ionizable compounds. The series has three components with
sharply different time scales. Emission plumes crossing the sensor produce large one-sided
spikes lasting seconds to a few minutes; the electronic baseline drifts slowly over hours with
temperature, humidity, and lamp condition; and high-frequency instrument noise rides on
top of both. The scientific interest lies exclusively in the spikes, but because each unit’s
baseline drifts idiosyncratically and the counts are not calibrated across units, the drifting
baseline must be estimated and removed before events can be flagged or compared across
sensors. Since the plumes are strictly one-sided excursions above the baseline, the baseline
is identified by a low conditional quantile of the series, making this a natural quantile trend
filtering problem in which, in contrast to the usual convention, the trend is the nuisance and
the residual carries the signal. We analyze one day of data, June 15, 2018, from sensor d
of the two collocated SPods in the EPA Rubbertown dataset accompanying Brantley et al.
(2020), aggregating the 86,400 raw 1-Hz observations into 10-second block means, which
yields n = 8,640 observations and attenuates the strong serial correlation of the 1-Hz noise
that the ALD working likelihood does not model.

Model and settings. We estimate the baseline at the three levels τ ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1}
with k = 2, so the prior favours piecewise quadratic trends, each level fitted independently
by the individual sampler of Section 2.1 on the standardized series. The sampler is run


                                              28
for 6,000 Gibbs iterations with a burn-in of 1,000, proximal parameter γ = 0.1, smoothing
parameter ϵ = 0.01, priors λ ∼ Gamma(1, 10) and σ ∼ IG(0.01, 0.01), and a step size
adapted during burn-in toward acceptance rate 0.5. Because SD (θ) is small for the smooth
baselines supported by these data, the rate parameter of the Gamma prior effectively pins
the posterior of λ near (n − k − 1)/10 ≈ 864, so the prior acts as a smoothness dial; we
return to this point below. As the frequentist benchmark we use the ADMM solver of the
detrendr package of Brantley et al. (2020) with the smoothing parameter selected by eBIC
over the grid λ ∈ exp{−2, . . . , 12}, which selects λ = 54.6 at τ = 0.01 and λ = 1096.6 at
τ ∈ {0.05, 0.1}. Following Brantley et al. (2020), event detection after baseline removal is a
threshold rule on the detrended series y − θ: ˆ︁ a point is classified as signal when it exceeds
the empirical 90th, 95th, or 99th percentile of the detrended data themselves, so that no
absolute calibration of the counts is required.

Results. Figure 3 overlays the three fitted baselines on the data for the proposed sam-
pler; the corresponding ADMM fits, and the detrended series they produce, are reported in
Appendix D.1. Both methods track the overnight rise, the large morning excursion around
05:00, and the slow afternoon decline, and both leave the plume spikes untouched above
the fit. The empirical calibration of the posterior median baselines is close to nominal: the
fraction of observations below the fitted curve is 0.005, 0.044, and 0.092 at τ = 0.01, 0.05, 0.1,
against 0.000, 0.043, and 0.092 for ADMM. The comparison at τ = 0.01 favours the Bayesian
fit: eBIC selects a twenty-fold smaller penalty at this level than at the other two, and the
resulting ADMM curve bends upward into the 05:00 plume and crosses the τ = 0.05 and
τ = 0.1 curves, whereas the posterior medians, whose smoothness is governed by the same
prior at all three levels, remain ordered and essentially parallel throughout the day. Figure 4
shows the detrended series with the three detection thresholds, and Figure 7b its ADMM
counterpart; with the τ = 0.05 baseline removed, the 95% threshold sits at 109 counts for the
proposed method (92 for ADMM) while the largest plumes exceed 400 counts, so the events
of interest separate cleanly from the noise floor under either baseline. Each level required
44–53 minutes of computation at n = 8,640 (6,000 iterations), against roughly four minutes
for the ADMM fit including the eBIC grid search; acceptance rates were 0.16, 0.39, and
0.50 at τ = 0.01, 0.05, 0.1. Mixing degrades at the most extreme level: the median effective
sample size of θ is 67 at τ = 0.1 and 39 at τ = 0.05, but drops to 3.5 at τ = 0.01, where
fewer than one observation in a hundred lies below the trend and the smoothed check-loss
gradient is nearly flat, so pointwise credible statements at τ = 0.01 carry substantial Monte
Carlo error at this chain length.

6.3    Growth Centiles for Dutch Children
Growth charts are the canonical use of quantile curves: a child’s height is read against the
population centiles at the same age, so the chart itself is a family of quantile curves indexed
by age. We use the Fifth Dutch Growth Study (Schönbeck et al., 2013), a nationwide cross-
sectional sample collected in 2009 and distributed with the mice package (van Buuren and
Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). After removing records without a height measurement the
data contain 4,817 boys and 5,190 girls aged 0 to 22 years, each measured once. Boys and
girls are analysed separately.
    As with the Munich rents, many children share a covariate value. We round age to the
nearest tenth of a year, which gives G = 220 equally spaced age slots for boys and G = 221 for
girls, and index the trend θ ∈ RG by slot; the likelihood sums the check loss over all children

7     Discussion
Bayesian-PMALA brings exact posterior sampling to quantile trend filtering while keeping
the proximal structure of the optimization problem. Under the normal–exponential repre-
sentation, the proximal map of the whole conditional posterior of the trend is a weighted
trend filtering problem, so no part of the check loss is smoothed and the Metropolis–Hastings
correction is exact. In the simulation study each competitor is most accurate on the bench-
mark that suits it, and Bayesian-PMALA is competitive with both on both signals, with one
default configuration for every signal, noise family and quantile level. It matches the locally
adaptive horseshoe sampler with a single global penalty per quantile, and it matches ADMM
while learning the penalty and the likelihood scale from the posterior instead of selecting a
penalty by an information criterion.
    The computational structure is the second difference from the Gibbs sampler of Onizuka
et al. (2024). Its supplied implementation forms a dense inverse and uses dense Gaussian
simulation at each iteration, also with its sparse option. Every linear system in Bayesian-
PMALA is banded, with bandwidth k +1 for every n. The metric M and the ADMM system
matrix change with (z, λ, σ) and are therefore factorized at each sweep, but one factorization

    The experiment of Section 5 clarifies what the global penalty buys. There the estimand
is a peak-free baseline at a low quantile and the observed series is contaminated by transient
peaks, so local adaptivity is a liability: the horseshoe’s coefficient-specific scales let the fit
follow each peak, which lowers the check loss on the contaminated data, raises the error
against the peak-free target, and removes from the detrended residuals part of the signal the
downstream detector is meant to find. A single penalty cannot bend locally, and Bayesian-
PMALA is far more accurate than the horseshoe sampler on that design and close to ADMM.
The design also shows the limit of what the likelihood can identify: the separation between
a smooth background and transient bursts is not in the data model y = θ + ε, so it has to
enter through the prior on λ. We use a second prior, fixed in advance and shared across
sample sizes, for data of this kind, and keep the weak default elsewhere; a hierarchy that
learns the separation itself, for instance by modelling the burst component explicitly, is a
natural extension.
    Treating the ALD scale σ as unknown is a substantive modeling choice. At fixed (λ, σ)
the conditional mode is the quantile trend filter with penalty λσ (8), so the posterior learns
the effective penalty through two parameters, the scale adapting to the noise level and λ to
the roughness of the trend, rather than asking one tuning parameter to absorb both roles.
Learning σ does not by itself guarantee frequentist coverage when the asymmetric Laplace


                                                                  34
likelihood is misspecified, a point also recognized by the calibration approach of Onizuka
et al. (2024).
    A single global penalty is a deliberate simplification relative to coefficient-specific shrink-
age, and the experiments do not establish a universally preferred prior or difference or-
der. The pointwise intervals are below nominal coverage at the outer quantile levels (Sec-
tion 3.1.2), and their calibration there requires further study.
    The joint sampler of Section 2.2 enforces non-crossing on every posterior draw at the
observed indices, and it does so without leaving the P-MALA framework: the order constraint
adds the indicator of a box to the negative log-conditional of each trend, which keeps it
convex, so only the proximal map changes and the Metropolis–Hastings correction keeps its
single-level form. The joint target is a constrained product of working posteriors for the
same data, not a joint likelihood for several quantiles, and how the constraint shifts each
level’s posterior relative to its single-level fit, in location and in interval width, remains to
be studied.
    Several extensions follow from the present work. Locally adaptive priors can be com-
bined with the same proximal update, since the conditional of the trend remains a weighted
quadratic plus a weighted ℓ1 penalty. Extension to spatially indexed signals requires gener-
alizing the difference operator to graphs, for which the systems are no longer banded and
sparse or multigrid solvers may serve a similar role. Finally, the convergence properties of
the full Gibbs sampler with continuous step-size adaptation remain to be established.

A.6     Step-size adaptation
After the warm-up, the step size is updated at every sweep by the Robbins–Monro recursion

                     log δt+1 = log δt + t−0.6 (αt − 0.5) ,     αt = min(1, α),

with α from (17), and δ is confined to [10−8 , 102 ]. The adaptation continues after burn-in,
because λ can still drift slowly in the retained sweeps and a frozen step size then lets the
acceptance rate drift with it. The gain t−0.6 decreases to zero, so the adaptation satisfies the
diminishing-adaptation condition of Roberts and Rosenthal (2007). In the 3000 simulation
runs of Section 3 the post-burn-in acceptance rate was between 0.47 and 0.52. In the joint
sampler each level has its own step size δj , updated by the same recursion with αt the
acceptance probability of its trend move, counted as zero when the proposal falls outside Bj .

A.7     Numerical safeguards
Four safeguards are part of the default algorithm and are applied identically in every run.
(i) Each draw zi is restricted to [10−8 , 104 ], and |yi − θi | is floored at 10−10 in (9); this keeps
the weights wi within a range in which M and the solver systems can be factorized, and
the upper bound is essentially never active because zi has an exponential prior with mean
σ. (ii) The difference-precision coefficient κλ2 /2 in (18) is capped at 108 . (iii) If a banded
Cholesky factorization fails because of rounding, a ridge ϵ maxi Aii is added to the diagonal,
starting from ϵ = 10−12 and multiplying ϵ by 100 up to five times; the ridge is part of the
solver, so the proximal map remains deterministic. (iv) A non-finite value of log α is treated
as a rejection, and any non-finite state stops the run with an error rather than propagating.
None of the 3000 runs of Section 3 stopped. The joint sampler adds one check: after every
update of a level, the trend is verified to lie in its box, and a violation stops the run.
    The Metropolis–Hastings argument of Section 2.1.5 covers (ii) and (iii), which change
the proposal and not the target, and the box check, which never triggers when the kernels
are implemented correctly. It does not cover (i): clipping the latent draws and flooring the
residual alter the ideal conditional of z slightly, and clipping is not sampling from a correctly
normalized truncated distribution. The exactness of the trend update is therefore a property
of that block, not a claim that the whole guarded implementation samples (7) or (20) exactly.

A.8     Run length
Each run uses 30000 sweeps and discards the first 10000, which include the 200 warm-up
sweeps, leaving 20000 retained draws. For the simulations at n = 500 the median wall time
per run was 152 seconds on one core of a Linux compute cluster; the median effective sample
size over the 20000 retained draws was 66 for the trend (median over coordinates) and 9.2
for log λ. From λ(0) = m the chain approaches the posterior level of λ more slowly as n
grows. In the design of Section 5, λ is stationary over the retained draws for n ≤ 2000,
with a median relative change below 1% between the two halves of the retained sweeps, and
a residual median change of 3–5% at n = 4000 for τ = 0.05 and 0.10. The application of
Section 6.1, with n = 8640, therefore uses longer chains.

A.9     Why each component was needed
The sampler was developed under one rule: a single default configuration, fixed before seeing
any data set and identical across signals, noise distributions and quantile levels, with every
candidate change evaluated on all 30 configurations of Section 3 before adoption rather than
on the configuration that motivated it.

Proximal map of the whole conditional. P-MALA requires the proximal map of the
entire negative log-density. Under the ALD likelihood that map contains the check loss, and
the alternative of smoothing the check loss and applying a proximal map to the penalty alone
did not perform well in preliminary experiments. The normal–exponential representation (5)
makes the conditional of θ a weighted quadratic plus the ℓ1 penalty, whose proximal map is
a weighted trend filtering problem, without approximating the likelihood.

Noise after the proximal map. Adding the Gaussian noise after T , as in (16), gives a
proposal with an explicit density. If the noise is added before a proximal map, the proposal
is the image of a Gaussian under a nonlinear map that concentrates mass on trends that are
sparse in Dθ; its density is not available, and a Gaussian density evaluated in its place does
not give a valid Metropolis–Hastings ratio for the stated posterior.

Curvature-matched metric. With M = √          In the noise in (16) is white, and its (k + 1)-th
differences have standard deviation of order δ in each of the m coordinates, each charged
by the penalty at rate λ. For the posterior values of λ at n = 500 this increases U by far

Rate of the Gamma prior. Because the posterior draws of the trend satisfy ∥Dθ∥1 ≈

must be small relative to ∥Dθ∥1 for λ to be learned. A unit rate is far from weak on this scale:
Gamma(1, 1) gives P (λ > 500) = e−500 , whereas Gamma(1, 10−3 ) gives P (λ > 500) ≈ 0.61.
