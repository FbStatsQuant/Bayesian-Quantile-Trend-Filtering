\section{Analysis of the assumptions}\label{app:assumptions}

This appendix states what Assumptions~\ref{ass:A} and \ref{ass:B} and the bound $V^*\le\bar V$ require, shows where each enters the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, and verifies them for the simulation settings of Section~\ref{sec:simulations}. Throughout, $F_{y_i}$ is the distribution function of $y_i$, $\theta^*_i$ is its $\tau$-quantile, $r=k+1$, $D=D^{(r)}$, $\mathrm{TV}(\theta)=n^k\|D\theta\|_1$, $V^*=\mathrm{TV}(\theta^*)$, and $\Delta_n^2(v)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\min\{|v_i|,v_i^2\}$.

\paragraph{Meaning of the assumptions.}
Assumption~\ref{ass:A} is a lower margin: the distribution function of $y_i$ grows at least linearly in a fixed neighborhood of the quantile, uniformly in $i$ and $n$. It holds when the density is bounded below near $\theta^*_i$, but it is weaker, since it constrains only increments of $F_{y_i}$. It is the condition of \citet{madrid2022risk}, and a lower density bound of this kind is also used for Bayesian linear quantile regression by \citet{sriram2013posterior}. Assumption~\ref{ass:B} is the matching upper margin. It excludes an atom at the quantile, so that $F_{y_i}(\theta^*_i)=\tau$, and it excludes densities that are unbounded near the quantile. Both conditions concern $F_{y_i}$ only near $\theta^*_i$, and neither requires a moment of $y_i$; Cauchy noise is allowed. They do not involve the signal. If $y_i=f(u_i)+\varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i\sim F_{u_i}$ and $q_\tau(u)=F_u^{-1}(\tau)$, then $F_{y_i}(\theta^*_i+\delta)-F_{y_i}(\theta^*_i)=F_{u_i}(q_\tau(u_i)+\delta)-F_{u_i}(q_\tau(u_i))$, which does not depend on $f$.

The assumptions exclude discrete or rounded data, a flat stretch of $F_{y_i}$ at the quantile, a noise scale that vanishes or grows without bound in $i$, and a level $\tau$ that depends on $n$. For every distribution $\underline f L\le\min\{\tau,1-\tau\}$, because the increment of $F_{y_i}$ is at most $1-\tau$ for $\delta>0$ and at most $\tau$ for $\delta<0$; the constant $\underline f$ therefore shrinks at extreme levels.

The condition $V^*\le\bar V$ is the bounded-variation class of \citet{tibshirani2014adaptive}. For $\theta^*_i=Q(i/n)$ with $Q$ having $k$ derivatives, $\mathrm{TV}(\theta^*)$ is the discrete analogue of the total variation of $Q^{(k)}$, and it stays bounded when $Q^{(k+1)}$ is integrable. Here $Q=f+q_\tau$ includes the noise quantile curve.

\paragraph{Role in the proof.}
Write $\delta=\theta-\theta^*$. The posterior depends on the data only through the check loss, and every probability in Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is under the true law of $y$. The expected excess check loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}(\delta)=\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^n\bigl\{\rho_\tau(y_i-\theta^*_i-\delta_i)-\rho_\tau(y_i-\theta^*_i)\bigr\}=\sum_{i=1}^n\int_0^{\delta_i}\bigl\{F_{y_i}(\theta^*_i+z)-\tau\bigr\}\,dz .
\]
Assumption~\ref{ass:B} gives $F_{y_i}(\theta^*_i)=\tau$, so the integrand has the sign of $z$ and $\mathcal{L}(\delta)\ge0$, with equality only at $\delta=0$ when $F_{y_i}$ is strictly increasing at $\theta^*_i$. This is why the posterior concentrates at the $\tau$-quantile whatever the distribution of the data.

Assumption~\ref{ass:A} gives the curvature of $\mathcal{L}$. For $|z|\le L$ the integrand is at least $\underline f|z|$ in absolute value, and for $|z|>L$ it is at least $\underline f L$ by monotonicity of $F_{y_i}$. Integrating,
\[
\mathcal{L}(\delta)\ge\tfrac12\,\underline f\,\min\{1,L\}\;n\,\Delta_n^2(\delta).
\]
The excess loss is quadratic in $\delta_i$ near zero and linear far from it, which is the shape of $\Delta_n^2$; this is why the contraction is measured in that loss. A large $\mathcal{L}(\delta)$ makes the numerator of the posterior small on the shells away from $\theta^*$.

Assumption~\ref{ass:B} gives the upper bound $\mathcal{L}(\delta)\le\tfrac12\bar f\|\delta\|_2^2$ for $\|\delta\|_\infty\le L$. Neighborhoods of $\theta^*$ defined by the excess loss therefore contain Euclidean balls, so the prior mass of a small ball bounds the denominator of the posterior from below. The proof first shows that the posterior puts all but exponentially small mass on a set where $\|\delta\|_\infty\le L$, so the two margins apply in every coordinate. The bound $\bar V$ enters the same step through the prior mass near a locally smoothed version of $\theta^*$. The constants $M$ and $C$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} depend only on $c$, $k$, $L$, $\underline f$, $\bar f$, and $\bar V$.

\paragraph{Verification for the simulation settings.}
The settings are the signals~\eqref{eq:sim_vs} and \eqref{eq:sim_s}, the three noise families of Section~\ref{sec:simulations}, the eleven levels $\tau\in\{0.05,0.25,0.50,0.75,0.95\}$ and $\{0.90,0.93,0.96,0.97,0.98,0.99\}$, and $k=2$. Assumptions~\ref{ass:A} and \ref{ass:B} depend only on the noise family and $\tau$, and $V^*$ depends on all three. The noise law at $i$ depends on $i$ only through $u_i=i/n\in(0,1]$ and varies continuously with it, so all constants below are uniform in $n$.

Table~\ref{tab:ass:margins} reports, for $L=0.1$, the smallest and largest value of $|F_u(q_\tau(u)+\delta)-F_u(q_\tau(u))|/|\delta|$ over $0<|\delta|\le L$ and $u\in(0,1]$, computed on a grid of 2000 values of $u$. These are admissible values of $\underline f$ and $\bar f$. Assumptions~\ref{ass:A} and \ref{ass:B} hold in all $33$ combinations of noise family and level. The smallest $\underline f$ is $0.031$, for mixed-normal noise at $\tau=0.99$. The largest $\bar f$ is $10.74$, for Beta noise at $\tau=0.05$. The global density bounds are $1.596$, $11$, and $0.564$ for the Gaussian, Beta, and mixed-normal families, so $\bar f$ cannot exceed these values.

For Gaussian noise the density at the quantile is $\varphi(z_\tau)/s(u)$ with $s(u)\in[0.25,0.5]$, which ranges from $0.798$ to $1.596$ at $\tau=0.50$. For mixed-normal noise it is a smooth mixture of two normal densities and stays between $0.036$ and $0.564$ at all levels. Under Beta noise $F_u(x)=1-(1-x)^{b}$ on $[0,1]$ with $b=11-10u\in[1,11)$, so the density is $b(1-x)^{b-1}$. Two features need a remark. First, the density jumps from zero to $b$ at the origin, and at small $\tau$ the quantile lies close to the jump: its distance from the origin is $0.0047$ at $\tau=0.05$ and $0.026$ and $0.061$ at $\tau=0.25$ and $0.50$, in each case as $u\to0$. A lower density bound would then hold only for $L<0.0047$. Assumption~\ref{ass:A} is stated for increments of $F_{y_i}$: once $\theta^*_i+\delta$ leaves the support, the increment equals $\tau$ and the ratio is $\tau/|\delta|\ge\tau/L$. This gives $\underline f=0.500$ at $\tau=0.05$, against a density of up to $10.5$ at the quantile. Second, $b\ge1$ on the whole design, so the density does not blow up at the upper endpoint, and the quantile stays at distance at least $1-\tau$ from it. Assumption~\ref{ass:B} would fail for a design that reaches $b<1$.

Table~\ref{tab:ass:tv} reports $V^*=n^2\|D^{(3)}\theta^*\|_1$ with $\theta^*_i=f(u_i)+q_\tau(u_i)$, as a range over the eleven levels. The quantile curves $q_\tau$ are smooth on $[0,1]$ in all three families, so $V^*$ converges to $\int_0^1|(f+q_\tau)'''(u)|\,du$ as $n\to\infty$. The values at $n=500$ and $n=16000$ are close, which is consistent with this limit, so $V^*\le\bar V$ holds in all $66$ combinations of signal, noise family, and level.

The noise quantile curve contributes little. For Gaussian noise $q_\tau(u)=s(u)z_\tau$ is a quadratic polynomial in $u$, which $D^{(3)}$ annihilates, so $V^*$ equals the total variation of $f$ and does not depend on $\tau$. For mixed-normal noise the contribution is between $0.17$ and $1.22$. For Beta noise it is between $9.5$ and $45.1$, which moves $V^*$ by about $-10\%$ to $+13\%$ for S and is negligible for VS. For S the limit is $16\pi^2=157.9$, the integral of $|f'''|$ for $f(u)=\sin(2\pi u)$. The value $7.3\times10^3$ for VS comes from the localized bump; it is about $46$ times the value for S, so the constants of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} are larger for VS.

The constants of the theorem also degrade where $\underline f$ is small, at $\tau\ge0.96$ and for Beta noise at $\tau=0.05$. The assumptions still hold there, so the rate is unchanged. The asymmetric Laplace likelihood is a working model and plays no role in the assumptions, which concern only the law of $y_i$; its consequences are discussed after Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
